The 5 Red Flags

Check any supplement in about a minute

The five, in order

This is the checklist from the clip. Screenshot it — it works on any product.
1
Hidden doses

A “proprietary blend” lets a brand hide how little of each ingredient is in there. If the label doesn’t print milligrams per ingredient, assume the expensive ones are traces.

2
No third-party seal

NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport or USP means an outside lab opened the tub. No seal means the only party vouching for the contents is the party selling them.

3
A claim a drug would need a trial for

“Clinically proven”, “boosts immunity”, “detoxifies”. Ask which trial, how many people, and what the primary outcome was. Post-hoc subgroup slicing is not proof.

4
The wrong plant, or an extra one

Botanical products get substituted and spiked. Species verification and a lot-specific certificate of analysis are the only things that catch it.

5
It’s a drug in disguise

Red yeast rice is a statin. Some “natural” products interact with prescriptions or carry a mandated warning. If it acts like a drug, it needs a doctor, not a shelf.

1 "Proprietary Blend"

The 5 red flags — "Proprietary blend"
  1. 1A blend total in mg with no per-ingredient breakdown. That is legal permission to hide the amounts.
  2. 2The impressive ingredient listed last — order is by weight, so last means least.
  3. 3A long ingredient list inside one blend, which guarantees most are traces.
  4. 4"Clinically dosed" claimed while the dose itself is hidden.
  5. 5No response when you email and ask for the per-ingredient amounts.
The case behind it

If the label shows a blend total (e.g. "5,000mg Performance Matrix") but hides each ingredient's dose — walk away. That 5,000mg can legally be mostly cheap filler like maltodextrin.

Documented: MusclePharm paid a $7,000,000 FTC settlement over exactly this. If a brand is proud of its doses, it prints them.

2 Amino / protein spiking

The 5 red flags — Protein spiking
  1. 1Free amino acids like glycine, taurine or creatine high in the ingredient list of a protein powder.
  2. 2Protein content that only adds up if you count nitrogen from non-protein sources.
  3. 3Priced well below comparable products with the same claimed protein per scoop.
  4. 4No amino acid profile published.
  5. 5"Protein blend" instead of a named protein source with a stated gram amount.
The case behind it

On protein powders, check the ingredient list for taurine, glycine, or creatine added high up. Labs measure protein by nitrogen — cheap aminos inflate the number without real protein.

Documented: Body Fortress paid $8,000,000 in 2014 after testing exposed this. Standard tests can't tell the difference — the ingredient list can.

3 No third-party testing seal

The 5 red flags — No third-party testing
  1. 1No NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport or USP seal. Supplements are not FDA-approved before sale.
  2. 2"Lab tested" with no lab named — in-house testing is the brand checking its own homework.
  3. 3No certificate of analysis available for the lot number on your tub.
  4. 4A seal image that is the brand's own graphic rather than a certifier's mark.
  5. 5If you compete in a tested sport, no certification means you carry the risk personally.
The case behind it

The FDA does not approve supplements before sale — that's been the law since 1994. Look for NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport, or USP Verified. No logo = you're the quality control.

Documented: when JAMA researchers tested 30 immune supplements, 17 labels didn't match the contents.

4 "Natural flavors"

The 5 red flags — "Natural flavors"
  1. 1"Natural flavors" on the label with nothing further specified. The term legally covers a long list of undeclared substances.
  2. 2A product marketed as clean or minimal that still uses it.
  3. 3No allergen statement, when flavour carriers can include allergens.
  4. 4"No artificial flavours" used as a health claim — natural and artificial say nothing about safety or dose.
  5. 5A flavour so strong it is doing the work the ingredients cannot.
The case behind it

Two words can legally hide dozens of undisclosed substances. The flavor's source must be natural — but the solvents, carriers and preservatives blended in with it don't have to be, and none of them are listed separately.

Documented: under FDA rule 21 CFR 101.22 a "natural flavor" is a trade-secret blend; the only thing that must be revealed inside it is a major allergen.

5 The serving-size trick

The 5 red flags — The serving-size trick
  1. 1Serving size of 3, 4 or 6 units. Every number on the panel is for all of them, not one.
  2. 2A tub whose "servings per container" quietly divides by that inflated serving.
  3. 3Cost per serving advertised, cost per gram of active never mentioned.
  4. 4Dose comparisons to competitors made per scoop rather than per gram.
  5. 5Do the arithmetic once: price divided by total grams of active. It usually reorders the shelf.
The case behind it

The serving size on a Supplement Facts panel is chosen by the brand, not the FDA. Watch two moves: a pre-workout that lists caffeine "per 1 scoop" but tells you to take two (halving the number you read, doubling the real dose), and "servings per container" — a "40-serving" tub can really be 20 at the dose you actually use. Everything (%DV, caffeine, creatine) is "per serving," so whoever sets the serving controls the story.

Documented: under 21 CFR 101.36 the manufacturer sets the supplement serving size, and there is no FDA pre-market approval (DSHEA, 1994). The FDA fixed this for regular food in its 2016 Nutrition Facts rule — a 20oz soda that once counted as 2.5 servings must now be labeled as 1 — but that reality-check never reached the supplement aisle.

6 "Clinically proven" / "clinically dosed"

The 5 red flags — "Clinically proven"
  1. 1"Clinically proven" with no study named. The phrase has no regulatory definition.
  2. 2"Clinically dosed" while the label hides the dose in a proprietary blend.
  3. 3A study on one ingredient used to sell a multi-ingredient formula.
  4. 4The trial is on the brand's own site only, not in a journal index.
  5. 5Sample size in the dozens, presented as if it settled the question.
The case behind it

"Clinically proven," "clinically studied" and "clinically dosed" have no FDA definition — supplements aren't approved before sale, so nobody verifies the phrase. Two moves to catch: a brand cites a study run on an ingredient, then puts a fraction of that studied dose in the tub; or the product actually gets tested and still fails. Ask: was the study run on THIS product at THIS dose, and did it beat placebo on its main outcome?

Documented: Prevagen was sold as "clinically shown" to improve memory. In Jan 2017 the FTC and the New York Attorney General sued maker Quincy Bioscience — its own 218-person, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Madison Memory Study showed no statistically significant benefit over placebo on any of its nine cognitive tasks, after which the company ran 30+ post-hoc subgroup analyses to find a "result."

7 Celebrity / influencer endorsement

The 5 red flags — Celebrity endorsements
  1. 1A famous face and no named scientist anywhere in the marketing.
  2. 2No #ad or paid-partnership disclosure, which is itself a regulatory breach.
  3. 3"I've been taking this for years" offered as the evidence.
  4. 4A discount code — the endorsement is a commission, not a recommendation.
  5. 5The celebrity's expertise is in something other than the thing being sold.
The case behind it

A famous face on a supplement is a paid ad, not evidence — a check for a post says nothing about what's in the tub. FTC rules require any paid or gifted endorsement to be disclosed clearly and conspicuously, not buried under Instagram's "more" button or a wall of hashtags. What actually protects the people who compete for a living isn't a celebrity — it's a third-party seal (NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport, USP) that screens the finished product against banned substances.

Documented: in 2020 the FTC charged Teami, LLC — a detox-tea brand that took in more than $15,000,000 while paying influencers such as Cardi B and Jordin Sparks to promote it on Instagram with the "paid" disclosure hidden under "more." The FTC secured a $15.2M judgment (suspended to $1M on ability to pay) and sent warning letters to all ten named influencers. By contrast, NSF Certified for Sport screens each product against 290 banned substances, and MLB and NHL clubs may provide players only Certified-for-Sport products (the NFL, PGA and LPGA recommend it).

8 Melatonin: the dose isn't the dose

The 5 red flags — Melatonin
  1. 1A gummy. Independent testing has found actual content ranging wildly from the label.
  2. 2A dose of 5-10 mg, when the studied effective range is far lower.
  3. 3Marketed for children with no paediatric dosing guidance.
  4. 4No CBD or other actives disclosed — some tested products contained them undeclared.
  5. 5Kept anywhere a child could mistake it for candy. Poison-centre calls for melatonin have risen sharply.
The case behind it

Melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, not a drug — so no one verifies its dose, identity or purity before it reaches the shelf (DSHEA, 1994). A gummy labeled 5mg can hold a fraction of that or several times more, and "sleep" blends may slip in CBD you never signed up for. It matters most in the candy-shaped products aimed at children.

Documented: a 2023 JAMA analysis of 25 melatonin gummies (Cohen et al., JAMA 2023;329(16):1401-1402) found 22 were inaccurately labeled — actual melatonin ranged from 74% to 347% of the amount on the label, and one product contained no detectable melatonin at all (but 31.3mg of CBD). Separately, the CDC reported that pediatric melatonin ingestions called into U.S. poison control rose 530% from 2012 to 2021 — more than 260,000 cases in a decade.

9 Heavy metals in protein powder

The 5 red flags — Protein powder
  1. 1No heavy-metal testing published. Independent testing has repeatedly found lead, cadmium and arsenic in protein powders.
  2. 2Plant-based or cocoa-flavoured, the two categories that test highest — plants take metals up from soil.
  3. 3No third-party certification (NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport, Clean Label Project).
  4. 4No lot number on the tub, so no certificate of analysis can ever be matched to what you bought.
  5. 5Multiple scoops a day for years. The concern is cumulative exposure, not a single serving.
The case behind it

Protein powders aren't screened for heavy metals before sale, and independent testing keeps finding lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury in popular products — with "organic" and plant-based often worse, not better. A Supplement Facts panel has no metals limit, so your only protection is a brand that third-party tests every batch and publishes the results.

Documented: the Clean Label Project (2018) tested 134 protein powders and found about 40% had elevated heavy metals; 75% of plant-based products tested positive for lead, and certified-organic powders were roughly twice as likely to contain heavy metals as non-organic.

10 Pure / powdered caffeine

The 5 red flags — Pure caffeine powder
  1. 1Caffeine sold as a loose powder rather than a measured tablet. A teaspoon can hold the caffeine of ~25 cups of coffee.
  2. 2Dosing instructions in fractions of a teaspoon. A kitchen scale cannot resolve the difference between a dose and an overdose.
  3. 3Bought in bulk from a marketplace. The FDA declared bulk sales to consumers unlawful after deaths.
  4. 4Kept anywhere a teenager can reach it.
  5. 5Stacked with pre-workout and coffee without counting the total.
The case behind it

Pure powdered caffeine is so concentrated that a single teaspoon is about 28 cups of coffee — and a safe dose is too small to measure with any kitchen spoon. It's still sold online as cheap "pre-workout." If a product is bulk caffeine powder you scoop yourself, walk away.

Documented: after deaths including 18-year-old Logan Stiner (2014, acute caffeine toxicity), the FDA acted against bulk pure/highly-concentrated caffeine sold to consumers (guidance finalized 2018) and warned distributors it is generally unlawful.

11 Titanium dioxide (E171)

The 5 red flags — Titanium dioxide (E171)
  1. 1E171 or titanium dioxide in the ingredients. The EU banned it as a food additive in 2022 after EFSA could no longer rule out genotoxicity.
  2. 2A bright white tablet coating or capsule — that is what it is there for. Colour, nothing else.
  3. 3Present in a product you take daily for years, where the exposure adds up.
  4. 4The brand sells in both the EU and the US with different formulas — check the EU version's list.
  5. 5No functional reason given for it. It does nothing for the active ingredient.
The case behind it

A white dye — E171 — added to tablets, capsule shells and coatings only to make pills look clean and uniform; it does nothing for you. Europe pulled it from all food and supplements after regulators couldn't rule out DNA damage, yet it's still legal and common in US pills.

Documented: EFSA concluded in 2021 that E171 can no longer be considered safe as a food additive (genotoxicity could not be excluded); the EU banned it in food and supplements from August 2022 (France banned it in 2020). Still permitted in the US.

12 Rancid (oxidized) fish oil

The 5 red flags — Fish oil
  1. 1No peroxide value or TOTOX figure anywhere on the pack or the brand's site.
  2. 2A bottle that has been sitting in a warm, lit shop display. Heat and light drive oxidation.
  3. 3Heavy citrus or mint flavouring, which is the standard way to mask a rancid smell.
  4. 4No 'best before' linked to a potency guarantee, only a manufacture date.
  5. 5Bite one open — if it tastes sharp, fishy or like paint, it is oxidised. Do not finish the bottle.
The case behind it

Omega-3 oil oxidizes — goes rancid — and the capsule hides the smell, so you can't catch it by sniffing. Rancid oil can hold far less active omega-3 than the label claims. Look for a third-party freshness / oxidation (TOTOX) test and a recent manufacture date, not just an expiry.

Documented: a 2015 peer-reviewed study (Scientific Reports) tested 32 omega-3 supplements — 83% exceeded recommended oxidation limits and only 3 met their labelled EPA/DHA. Industry-funded follow-ups dispute how widespread this is, so treat it as "test your brand," not "all fish oil is rancid."

13 Spiked with hidden prescription drugs

The 5 red flags — Hidden prescription drugs
  1. 1Weight loss, sexual performance or bodybuilding — the three categories the FDA finds adulterated most.
  2. 2An effect that feels pharmaceutical. If it works like a drug, it may contain one.
  3. 3Sold only through a marketplace or a single-page site with no company address.
  4. 4The brand or product appears in the FDA's tainted products database — it is searchable and free.
  5. 5"All natural" used as the main claim, which is what these products lead with.
The case behind it

"All-natural" is not a safety guarantee. Some weight-loss, "muscle-building" and sexual-enhancement supplements are secretly spiked with real pharmaceuticals that never appear on the label — most often sibutramine (a weight-loss drug pulled from the U.S. market in 2010 for heart-attack and stroke risk), sildenafil, or synthetic steroids. If a product promises drug-like results with no prescription, that is the red flag.

Documented: the FDA’s own Health Fraud database logged 1,068 supplements tainted with undeclared drugs (2007–2021; Tucker et al., JAMA Network Open 2018) — about 85% of adulterated weight-loss products hid sibutramine. See the FDA “Tainted Products Marketed as Dietary Supplements” public list.

14 Collagen you swallow can't target your skin

The 5 red flags — Collagen
  1. 1Any claim that swallowed collagen goes to your skin, joints or hair specifically.
  2. 2No mention that it is digested into amino acids like every other protein.
  3. 3Before-and-after photos over any measured outcome.
  4. 4"Type I and III" presented as if the type survives digestion.
  5. 5Priced far above whey for a protein with a worse amino acid profile.
The case behind it

Eat collagen and your gut breaks it into amino acids like any protein — your body decides where they go; you can't route it to your face or joints on demand. "Clinically proven to firm skin" gets no FDA pre-market check, and the studies behind those claims are often small or industry-funded. Your body already builds its own collagen from dietary protein plus vitamin C.

Documented: Vital Proteins and HUM Nutrition ("Collagen Love") both face false-advertising class actions over "clinically proven" skin and hair claims (DSHEA 1994 — no FDA pre-market approval of supplement claims).

15 Greens powder isn't a serving of vegetables

The 5 red flags — Greens powders
  1. 1"Equivalent to X servings of vegetables" — a claim with no legal or nutritional definition.
  2. 2Fibre content near zero, which is most of what vegetables actually give you.
  3. 3A proprietary blend, so the greens may be a dusting behind the cheap fillers.
  4. 4Sold as a replacement for eating vegetables rather than an addition.
  5. 5No third-party heavy-metal testing, which matters for concentrated plant material.
The case behind it

"Equal to X servings of veg" has no FDA definition. A ~12g scoop keeps some micronutrients but loses the fiber, water and volume of real produce, and the greens are buried in a proprietary blend so you can't see the tiny doses.

Documented: ConsumerLab testing found fruit/vegetable powders are not a substitute for produce — at best a daily serving meets about one-fifth of the adult requirement, most provide only 2–10g of actual produce, and some contain more sugar or tapioca syrup than fruits or vegetables (DSHEA 1994).

16 “Testosterone booster” claims

The 5 red flags — Testosterone boosters
  1. 1Any claim to raise testosterone from a pill you can buy without a prescription.
  2. 2A proprietary blend hiding how little of each herb is in there.
  3. 3Reviews of top sellers have repeatedly found most ingredients have no supporting human data at the doses used.
  4. 4"Free testosterone", "bioavailable", "optimises" — words that sound clinical and measure nothing.
  5. 5No blood test before or after. If you never measured it, you cannot know it moved.
The case behind it

90% of “T booster” supplements claim to raise testosterone, but a peer-reviewed review of 50 top sellers found only 24.8% of the ingredients had any data showing an increase — and 10.1% had data showing they LOWER it. The common ingredient D-aspartic acid dropped testosterone about 12.5% at 6 g/day in resistance-trained men. Many also megadose vitamins and minerals past the FDA’s tolerable upper limit.

Documented: Clemesha, Thaker & Samplaski, World Journal of Men’s Health 2020 (50 products); Melville, Siegler & Marshall, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 2015 (D-aspartic acid).

17 Multivitamins for disease prevention

The 5 red flags — Daily multivitamins
  1. 1Marketed for preventing disease or living longer. Large trials have not shown that in well-fed people.
  2. 2Doses far above the daily requirement for things you already get from food.
  3. 3Taken instead of fixing a specific, tested deficiency.
  4. 4"Insurance policy" framing that discourages ever getting bloods done.
  5. 5Contains iron you were never told you needed.
The case behind it

For healthy, well-nourished adults, a daily multivitamin doesn’t extend life or prevent disease. The largest analysis to date — 390,124 adults over 20+ years — found NO lower risk of death. The US Preventive Services Task Force (2022) found insufficient evidence they prevent heart disease or cancer, and recommends AGAINST beta-carotene (raises lung-cancer risk) and vitamin E.

Documented: Loftfield et al., JAMA Network Open 2024 (390,124 adults); US Preventive Services Task Force, JAMA 2022.

18 Biotin wrecks your blood tests

The 5 red flags — Biotin (hair, skin & nails)
  1. 1Doses in the thousands of micrograms — hundreds of times the daily requirement.
  2. 2No warning that biotin interferes with laboratory blood tests.
  3. 3Taken before a hospital visit, GP appointment or blood draw without telling anyone.
  4. 4Any cardiac test in the picture. Biotin can push troponin results low and mask a heart attack.
  5. 5Marketed for hair growth in people with no diagnosed deficiency.
The case behind it

Hair, skin & nail supplements megadose biotin (vitamin B7) — the FDA says up to 650x the amount your body needs. That much biotin interferes with common lab tests, pushing results falsely high or falsely low depending on the test. The most dangerous is a falsely LOW troponin, the marker doctors use to catch a heart attack. Tell your doctor you take it and stop before any bloodwork.

Documented: FDA Safety Communication “The FDA Warns that Biotin May Interfere with Lab Tests” (2017, updated 2019) — the FDA received a report of one patient who DIED after a falsely low troponin result on a test with known biotin interference. Biotin deficiency is rare (adult adequate intake ~30mcg/day, NIH).

19 Probiotic “proven” health claims

The 5 red flags — Probiotic health claims
  1. 1"Scientifically proven" to regulate digestion or immunity. Those exact claims drew a $21m FTC settlement.
  2. 2No strain named. Effects are strain-specific.
  3. 3No CFU count guaranteed at end of shelf life, only at manufacture.
  4. 4A claim about a disease, which a food product may not legally make.
  5. 5Refrigeration ignored, when the strain requires it.
The case behind it

“Supports immunity / digestion” on a probiotic is usually unproven — these health claims aren’t approved before sale, and the label rarely tells you the three things that matter: the exact strain, the live-culture dose (CFU), and whether the bacteria survive stomach acid. A brand can even trademark an official-sounding bacterium name to make marketing feel like science.

Documented: in Dec 2010 the FTC and 39 state attorneys general charged Dannon with deceptive ads for Activia and DanActive (“scientifically proven” to relieve irregularity / prevent colds). Dannon dropped or qualified the claims and paid the states $21 million; it had trademarked the fanciful names “Bifidus Regularis” and “L. casei Immunitas.”

20 Fat burners & liver failure

The 5 red flags — Fat burners
  1. 1A stimulant blend with no per-ingredient dose printed.
  2. 2Any ingredient that has been the subject of a recall under a previous name.
  3. 3No liver warning. Documented clusters have ended in transplants and deaths.
  4. 4Sold for rapid weight loss, the category with the worst adulteration record.
  5. 5Stacked on top of coffee and pre-workout without counting total stimulant load.
The case behind it

"Fat burner" / weight-loss pills are a repeat offender for acute liver injury — some have sent healthy users into hepatitis, a transplant, or death within weeks. No supplement is FDA-approved before sale, so a recall only comes after people are hurt. Be extra wary of aggressive fat-burner blends and any product reformulated right after an ingredient got banned.

Documented: in 2013 the FDA forced a recall of OxyELITE Pro (USPlabs) after 50+ cases of acute liver injury — a Hawaii cluster had 2 liver transplants and 1 death (CDC MMWR 2013; NIH LiverTox). In 2009 the FDA logged 23 serious liver injuries including one death of a 19-year-old, and Iovate recalled 14 Hydroxycut products.

21 Vitamin B6: megadoses damage nerves

The 5 red flags — Vitamin B6
  1. 1A dose in the hundreds of milligrams. Nerve damage is documented at sustained high intake.
  2. 2"Water-soluble so you just pee out the excess" — not true for B6.
  3. 3Hidden inside an energy drink or a B-complex you also take separately.
  4. 4No upper limit stated on the label.
  5. 5Tingling, numbness or unsteadiness treated as unrelated.
The case behind it

"Water-soluble = harmless" is a myth. In high or stacked doses, vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) slowly damages sensory nerves — tingling, burning, numb hands and feet, and loss of balance. It's often hidden inside magnesium and sleep supplements you didn't know contained it, so the dose stacks up without you noticing.

Documented: Schaumburg et al., NEJM 1983 — 7 people taking 2,000-6,000mg/day developed sensory neuropathy with ataxia. The US tolerable upper limit is 100mg/day, set specifically because of nerve damage; Australia's TGA now requires a nerve-damage warning above 10mg/day and notes neuropathy can begin below 50mg/day; EFSA cut its safe upper limit to 12mg/day in 2023. This is about megadoses/stacking — not normal dietary B6.

22 Turmeric / curcumin & your liver

The 5 red flags — Turmeric extract
  1. 1A concentrated extract rather than the spice. The liver cases involve extracts.
  2. 2Piperine or black pepper added to boost absorption.
  3. 3No liver warning despite documented hepatitis cases.
  4. 4Taken daily for months with no check-in.
  5. 5Yellowing, dark urine or right-side pain treated as anything other than stop and see a doctor.
The case behind it

Turmeric is the best-selling botanical supplement in the US, but it's tied to drug-induced liver injury — and the risk rides on the label. "Enhanced absorption" or "high-potency" blends add piperine (black pepper) that can raise curcumin absorption up to 20x, turning a mild spice into a concentrated dose. Supplements get no FDA pre-market testing (DSHEA 1994). Plain culinary turmeric on food isn't the concern; high-dose, absorption-boosted capsules are.

Documented: Halegoua-DeMarzio et al., The American Journal of Medicine (2023) reported 10 turmeric-associated liver-injury cases from the NIH Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network (2004–2022, most since 2017) — 5 hospitalized and 1 death from acute liver failure; injury was hepatocellular with an HLA-B*35:01 genetic link. Herbal & dietary supplements now account for ~20% (1 in 5) of US drug-induced liver injury cases, up from ~7% in 2004.

23 Store-brand herbal supplements

The 5 red flags — Store-brand herbals
  1. 1A supermarket or pharmacy own-brand herbal at a suspiciously low price.
  2. 2No DNA or species verification offered. A NY Attorney General investigation found only 21% contained the labelled plant.
  3. 3Fillers like rice or houseplant material found where the herb should be.
  4. 4No lot-specific certificate of analysis.
  5. 5No named third-party lab anywhere on the packaging.
The case behind it

Store-brand herbal pills (echinacea, ginseng, St. John's wort, saw palmetto) may not contain the herb on the label at all. In New York's DNA investigation, only 21% of store-brand products contained DNA of the plant printed on the front — the rest were undisclosed fillers like rice, wheat, even houseplant. With no FDA pre-market approval (DSHEA 1994), nobody verifies the herb is actually in the bottle before it's sold.

Documented: the NY Attorney General (Feb 3, 2015) sent cease-and-desist letters to GNC, Target, Walmart and Walgreens after DNA barcoding; per-retailer match rates were Walmart 4%, Walgreens 18%, GNC 22%, Target 41%. In 2016 NBTY (maker for Walmart/Walgreens) signed a nationwide reform agreement. (DNA barcoding is debated for processed extracts, but the AG action and settlement are documented.)

24 BCAAs when protein is adequate

The 5 red flags — BCAAs
  1. 1Sold to someone already eating adequate protein — that is most people who buy them.
  2. 2Three amino acids priced like a complete protein.
  3. 3"Anti-catabolic" claims with no measured outcome.
  4. 4Flavoured water marketed as a training necessity.
  5. 5Compare it against whey per gram of leucine; the maths usually ends the argument.
The case behind it

If you already hit your protein target, BCAA supplements are mostly wasted money. Building muscle needs all 9 essential amino acids; BCAAs supply only 3. A complete protein (meat, eggs, dairy, whey) already gives all 9 — plus more leucine than a BCAA scoop.

Documented: Jackman et al. (Front. Physiol. 2017) — BCAAs alone produced a sub-maximal muscle-protein-synthesis response vs. whole protein/EAAs. Wolfe (JISSN 2017) — BCAAs alone cannot maximally or sustainably raise muscle protein synthesis without the other essential amino acids. Not dangerous — just unnecessary if protein intake is adequate.

25 Vitamin C for preventing colds

The 5 red flags — Vitamin C for colds
  1. 1"Boosts immunity" or "prevents colds". The Cochrane review does not support prevention in the general population.
  2. 2Mega-doses of 1,000 mg or more taken daily as routine.
  3. 3Effervescent formats with substantial added sodium.
  4. 4Sold as treatment once you already feel ill.
  5. 5No mention that the measurable effect is a modest shortening of duration, not prevention.
The case behind it

A daily vitamin C tablet won't stop you catching a cold — the “immune boost” is mostly marketing. Regular supplementation does not reduce cold incidence in the general population; it only shortens a cold slightly (~1 day), and mega-dosing after you're already sick shows no consistent benefit.

Documented: Hemilä & Chalker, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2013 — regular vitamin C did not lower cold incidence in the general community (~11,000 participants); reduced duration ~8% (adults)/14% (children); therapeutic dosing at onset had no consistent effect. Incidence fell only in people under extreme short-term physical stress (e.g. marathon runners).

26 Ashwagandha & liver injury

The 5 red flags — Ashwagandha
  1. 1No liver warning. Regulators in several countries have flagged liver injury cases.
  2. 2Taken alongside thyroid medication without telling a doctor.
  3. 3No extract ratio or standardisation stated.
  4. 4Marketed for stress or sleep with no time limit on use.
  5. 5Taken during pregnancy — it is contraindicated.
The case behind it

The stress/cortisol “adaptogen” ashwagandha has documented cases of liver injury — jaundice (yellow skin/eyes), nausea, lethargy and itching, typically after 2–12 weeks of use. It's idiosyncratic (rare, not everyone), but there is no warning on the label. Stop and see a doctor if you notice yellowing eyes, dark urine or unusual fatigue.

Documented: Björnsson HK et al., “Ashwagandha-induced liver injury: a case series from Iceland and the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network,” Liver International 2020 — 5 patients, all jaundiced, latency 2–12 weeks, liver tests normalised in 1–5 months. Further case series from India (2023); NIH LiverTox lists ashwagandha as a cause of clinically apparent liver injury.

27 Gummy vitamins & dose accuracy

The 5 red flags — Gummy vitamins
  1. 1A gummy claiming a full multivitamin. Heat-sensitive vitamins do not survive the gummy process well.
  2. 2No potency guarantee at end of shelf life — gummies degrade faster than tablets.
  3. 3Sugar in the first three ingredients while sold as a health product.
  4. 4Serving size of 2-3 gummies, with the label percentages quietly calculated on all of them.
  5. 5No third-party lot testing. Independent testing repeatedly finds gummy potency off-label in both directions.
The case behind it

Gummy vitamins fail dose testing far more often than tablets — in both directions. They're made with heat and moisture and keep degrading in the jar, so brands add 20–50% overages: the same product can be over the label early in shelf life and under it later. They also carry 2–8g of sugar each. If the dose matters (vitamin D, folic acid, anything for kids), a tablet or capsule is the safer format.

Documented: ConsumerLab testing found roughly 80% of gummy vitamins failed its quality tests and are prone to degradation. In a multivitamin review, all four gummy multivitamins had potency inaccuracies — one contained significantly less vitamin D than claimed, three contained nearly double their stated folic acid. Vitamin C in a gummy can lose 30–50% potency within 12 months vs 24–36 months for a dry tablet.

28 Vitamin D megadoses

The 5 red flags — Vitamin D megadoses
  1. 1The bottle says IU, not mcg — 10,000 IU sounds smaller than 250 mcg. Convert before you judge the dose.
  2. 2A daily dose above 4,000 IU with no blood test behind it. That is the tolerable upper limit for adults.
  3. 3"Loading dose" or "weekly mega-dose" language on a shelf product.
  4. 4Sold next to calcium with no warning — together they are what drives blood calcium up.
  5. 5No mention that D is fat-soluble. It builds up over weeks; stopping today does not undo a month of overdosing.
The case behind it

Vitamin D is fat-soluble — you don't pee out the excess. Mega-dose products (self-prescribed “more is better”) are documented to cause hypercalcaemia and acute kidney injury, and because its half-life is about 2 months, the toxicity drags on for weeks. Normal doses are fine — but get a blood test instead of guessing, and be wary of very high-IU products.

Documented: BMJ Case Reports 2022 — a 73-year-old man self-medicating supplements presented with life-threatening hypervitaminosis D; his vitamin D level was seven times the level required for sufficiency, with acute kidney injury and very high calcium. Also Military Medicine 2024: vitamin D abuse “in pursuit of gains” causing acute kidney injury; published cases of oxalate nephropathy from vitamin D intoxication.

29 Green tea EXTRACT (not the tea)

The 5 red flags — Green tea extract
  1. 1Concentrated EGCG in a capsule. Brewed green tea is not the thing carrying the safety signal.
  2. 2Found inside a fat burner or "metabolism" blend, where you do not notice the dose.

The 5 red flags — Iron supplements
  1. 1No accidental-overdose warning on the pack. That warning is legally mandated for a reason.
  2. 2Stored anywhere a child can reach. Iron is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in under-6s.

35 Activated charcoal & your meds

The 5 red flags — Activated charcoal
  1. 1Sold as a daily "detox". It is an emergency-medicine adsorbent, not a wellness habit.
  2. 2No warning that it binds medication. It does not distinguish between a toxin and your prescription.
  3. 3Taken within a couple of hours of any drug, including contraceptives.
  4. 4In food or drink form, where the dose is unmeasurable.
  5. 5"Removes toxins" with no toxin named. Your liver and kidneys already do this continuously.
The case behind it

Activated charcoal is a hospital antidote for poisoning because it adsorbs drugs in your gut and stops them reaching your bloodstream — and it does that non-selectively. It can bind hormonal contraceptives, antidepressants, anti-epileptics, beta blockers, diabetes medicines and painkillers. Dose and timing matter (medical doses ~50 g vs typically <1 g in detox drinks), but if you take daily medication, separate it by at least 2 hours. There's no evidence it “detoxes” anything your liver and kidneys don't.

Documented: activated charcoal is standard emergency treatment for acute poisoning precisely because it prevents systemic absorption of drugs from the GI tract; clinicians advise separating charcoal from medication by ~2 hours. Contraception that bypasses the gut (patch, ring, IUD) is unaffected.

The 5 red flags — Food intolerance tests
  1. 1The test measures IgG. IgG to a food is a normal marker of exposure — often of tolerance, not intolerance.

The 5 red flags — Supplements alongside prescriptions
  1. 1St John's wort taken with anything. It speeds up the liver enzymes that clear many medications.
  2. 2Vitamin K supplements while on warfarin — it works directly against the drug.

If you try it, glycinate is the gentle, well-absorbed form; oxide is cheap and mostly a laxative. Skip the proprietary “sleep blends” where the dose is hidden.

55 Homeopathic doesn't mean a safe, zero dose

  1. 1A homeopathic product for infants that still lists a real active plant (like belladonna) — "diluted" is not the same as "consistent".

59 Yohimbine: real drug, roulette dose

The 5 red flags — Yohimbe / yohimbine
  1. 1"Yohimbe bark" listed with no milligrams of yohimbine — actual content ranges wildly from the label.
  2. 2Yohimbine buried inside a proprietary blend, so you can't know the dose.
  3. 3A stimulant fat-burner or "libido" product stacked with caffeine — compounding the cardiovascular hit.
  4. 4No warning about heart-rate, blood-pressure or anxiety effects anywhere on the label.
  5. 5You have a heart condition, high blood pressure or an anxiety disorder — this is a hard pass.
The case behind it

Yohimbine is a real compound that was once a prescription drug — which is the problem, because the supplement dose is unknown. A 2016 analysis of 49 yohimbe/yohimbine products found actual content from about a quarter of the label to nearly half again over it, with roughly three-quarters listing no amount at all; more than 40% held pharmaceutical-level doses. Yohimbine can cause a racing heart, high blood pressure and anxiety. No milligrams on the label, or hidden in a blend, means you can't dose it safely.
